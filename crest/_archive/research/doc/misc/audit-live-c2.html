CREST live-web citation audit — C2 (counterfactual reasoning), 10 items

Scope: the 10 items whose id starts with C2_ in question/crest_challenges.audit.jsonl (working copy; no .jsonl file was edited by this audit). Method: live WebSearch + WebFetch against primary sources (arXiv, Crossref, PubMed/PMC, legislation.gov.uk statute PDFs, CourtListener/Justia, publisher pages, MacTutor, Wikipedia as secondary corroboration only). Only real-world factual claims were graded; the counterfactual reasoning itself is not fact-checkable and was left alone. Date: 2026-07-30.

Items audited: C2_arts_humanities_11, C2_biology_medicine_06, C2_biology_medicine_34, C2_economics_business_08, C2_economics_business_36, C2_engineering_10, C2_law_policy_09, C2_law_policy_37, C2_materials_chemistry_07, C2_materials_chemistry_35.

0. Headline findings

  1. C2_engineering_10 — WRONG figure, in known_answers. The Hippke citation itself is clean (venue, year, arXiv id, submission month all correct), but the paper's ~400,000 t fuel figure is for a 45 t Apollo-class mission, not a "Hubble-class payload". Hippke never mentions Hubble; his telescope example is JWST at 6.2 t → 55,000 t. This is a known_answers error and must be fixed before release.
  2. C2_biology_medicine_06 — two hard author-list errors, both in known_answers. Rudensky is not an author of Aluvihare et al., Nat Immunol 2004 (third author is Kallikourdis); Dunn is not an author of the 2011 Science immunoediting paper (Schreiber, Old & Smyth). Nothing fabricated — both papers exist — but the attributions are wrong.
  3. C2_arts_humanities_11 — one substantively wrong example. Melozzo da Forlì (c. 1438–1494) is offered as foreshortening that predates the vanishing-point construction; he postdates Brunelleschi and Alberti by two generations and works inside the perspective tradition.
  4. C2_economics_business_08 — the prior pass's removals held. A byte-level scan of the item found no %, no GDP, no billion/trillion, no Cox, no Edward anywhere in query/known_answers/expert_baseline/hard_rules. The suspected-fabricated "5-20% of U.S. GDP" statistic and the "Edward Cox 1855" reference are gone and nothing comparable replaced them. Its ~19 legal-history and finance-theory claims verify remarkably well, including against the primary statute text of the Companies Act 1879. Two residual soft spots: the 1863-1933 double-liability date range, and an unsourced characterisation of Murray Rothbard.
  5. C2_engineering_10 also carries an unsourced number and a wrong hard rule. The "sustained ~16 g for a minute may be lethal" figure traces to a single uncited Wikipedia sentence that carries a live citation-needed tag; the standard primary reference (Eiband, NASA Memo 5-19-59E, 1959) explicitly says no lethal boundary has been established. Separately, hard_rules[1]'s "LOX/LH2 vacuum Isp <= ~450 s" is beaten by flown hardware (RL10B-2 = 465.5 s) — a rule that could penalise a correct agent answer.
  6. Nothing fabricated anywhere in C2. Every cited paper, book, statute and case exists with the stated author/title. The failure mode in this category is misattribution, date/figure drift, and absolutes that the comparative literature contradicts — not invention. C2 is markedly cleaner than a category with invented citations would be, but four items need edits before release.

Verbatim from the paper (via ar5iv full text):

"For a mass ratio of 83, the minimum rocket (1 t to v_esc) would carry 9,000 t of fuel on Kepler-20 b … To lift a more useful payload of 6.2 t as required for the James Webb Space Telescope on Kepler-20 b, the fuel mass would increase to 55,000 t … For a classical Apollo moon mission (45 t), the rocket would need to be considerably larger, ~400,000 t."

(For comparison the paper gives Saturn V a total mass of ~3,050 t for the same 45 t lunar payload. Hubble's actual launch mass is ~11 t — i.e. between Hippke's two bracketing cases, so the substituted label is wrong in both directions: wrong payload class and a figure that belongs to a payload 4× heavier.)

Exact replacement (old → new) — in C2_engineering_10.known_answers[2]:

  • OLD: (e.g., a Hubble-class payload from a 10-Earth-mass world would need on the order of ~400,000 t of fuel)
  • NEW: (e.g., from a 10-Earth-mass world like Kepler-20 b, a 6.2 t James-Webb-class payload would need ~55,000 t of fuel and a 45 t Apollo-class mission ~400,000 t, against the Saturn V's ~3,050 t for the same 45 t)

This matters: the item's whole design is to cap cheap surprise by pre-listing Hippke's headline result as KNOWN. A judge holding a wrong quantitative anchor could mark a correct agent answer (55,000 t for a telescope-class payload) as a factual error.

known_answers[9] — Rothbard:

  • OLD: Critical literature argues limited liability enables negative corporate externalities through risk-shifting (e.g., Murray Rothbard's position that liability for torts should be full/unlimited); the aggregate magnitude of these externalities is contested and not precisely quantified in the literature.

15. Overall assessment: are C2's baselines trustworthy?

Broadly yes, with four items needing edits first. The verdict distribution — 110 CONFIRMED, 4–5 WRONG, 22 IMPRECISE, 4 UNVERIFIABLE, 0 FABRICATED — is a healthy profile for a benchmark of this size. Every paper, book, statute, treaty and case cited across the ten items exists with the stated author and title. There is no invented-citation problem in C2. That is the single most important finding, because a fabricated known_answers entry would silently corrupt the novelty baseline in a way no agent could detect or contest.

The real failure mode is misattribution and figure drift, and it clusters in known_answers — the worst possible place. known_answers is what every agent answer is scored against for New/Surprise, so an error there does not merely mislead a reader; it inverts the grade. Three concrete instances:

  1. C2_engineering_10's Hippke figure. The item's entire design is to pre-declare Hippke's result KNOWN so agents cannot earn cheap Surprise. But the pre-declared anchor is wrong: an agent that correctly reports 55,000 t for a telescope-class payload now disagrees with the rubric and can be marked down for a correct fact. The same item's hard_rules[1] sets an Isp ceiling (450 s) that real flown hardware exceeds (465.5 s), so an agent citing the RL10B-2 correctly could be scored as violating a hard rule. Both are self-inflicted scoring hazards, not merely cosmetic errors.
  2. C2_biology_medicine_06's two author lists. Rudensky-for-Kallikourdis and Dunn-on-the- 2011-Science-paper are the classic LLM-generated-citation signature: right paper, right year, right journal, plausible-but-wrong senior author drawn from the topic's most famous name. Worth treating as a systematic risk indicator for the rest of the benchmark.
  3. C2_arts_humanities_11's Melozzo example. Cited to prove foreshortening predates the vanishing-point construction, but Melozzo works two generations after Alberti and inside the perspective tradition. The claim it is meant to support is true; the evidence offered refutes itself.

A distinct, subtler category: absolutes that the comparative literature contradicts. C2_biology_medicine_34 states that anucleate red cells lack the death machinery that nucleated ones have — but mammalian RBCs undergo regulated eryptosis, and the item's query builds its Plasmodium/apoptosis thread on exactly that premise. Similarly "nucleated erythrocytes are the norm in birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish" has real convergent exceptions (plethodontid salamanders, Maurolicus). These are not citation errors; they are over-confident biology, and a well-read agent that correctly raises eryptosis would be penalised as off-premise.

On the flagged economics item, the prior pass's work held cleanly. A programmatic scan found no percentage, no GDP reference, and no trace of "Edward Cox" anywhere in C2_economics_business_08. Nothing comparable crept in to replace them. Its remaining ~19 claims are unusually well-sourced for legal history — I verified the Companies Act 1879's re-registration and reserve-liability provisions against the primary statute text, and the Butler 1911 quote, the four law-review citations, Modigliani-Miller and Markowitz all check out precisely. The only residue worth acting on is the Rothbard parenthetical, which sits in the same sentence as the epistemic hedge the prior removal left behind — the same signature as the excised "Edward Cox" line, and my recommendation is to cut or rewrite it.

No item is entirely error-free, but two carry no WRONG verdicts at all: C2_law_policy_37 and C2_economics_business_36 need only optional precision work (restore Frye's 97%/94% split; scope the loading-cost claim to loading rather than freight generally). The error rate tracks citation density, which is the expected pattern and suggests retrieval noise rather than systematic invention.

A methodological finding worth carrying to the other categories. Three claims in this audit's own first draft were wrong because Wikipedia was wrong and I trusted it: the Ideal-X's container length (33 ft, not 35 ft) and departure port (Port Newark, not Port Newark–Elizabeth); Levinson's cost pair ($5.83/15.8¢, not $5.86/16¢); and the ISO container timeline, whose widely-copied "ISO/R 1897" is a typo for ISO/R 1894:1970 that originates in Rushton et al., Handbook of Logistics (2004) and propagates through Wikipedia. Each was caught only by going to the primary document (Princeton's hosted chapter of The Box, ISO's own deliverables catalogue). Any C2-style claim currently resting on a Wikipedia corroboration should be treated as provisional; the primary sources exist and are reachable.

Recommendation. Apply the exact replacements in §§1 and 3–10 above — 4 WRONG fixes plus 17 precision fixes plus 2 recommended cuts. Do not publish C2_engineering_10, C2_biology_medicine_06 or C2_arts_humanities_11 unedited. After those edits, C2's baselines are trustworthy enough to score against.

Two audit caveats. (a) The session's WebSearch budget (200 calls) was exhausted partway through; the majority of verdicts were therefore established by direct WebFetch against primary sources — arguably stronger evidence than search snippets, but it meant some low-value claims were corroborated via Wikipedia rather than a primary document, and those are labelled as such.
