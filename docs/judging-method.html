How creativity is judged

Two AI judges compare answers in pairs. Each chooses one overall winner, guided by New, Useful and Surprising. The ranking is the share of comparisons each system wins.

One overall choice

New

What is original about the central contribution? Originality can lie in a new relationship between familiar ideas. A judge’s failure to recall prior work does not establish novelty.

Useful

How well does the contribution serve the task? Assess its reasoning and support against the question’s premises and the level of development requested.

Surprising

How does the approach differ from what a competent answer would normally propose? Credit unexpected ideas when they contribute meaningfully to the task.

The judge weighs these qualities together, explains both answers and their tradeoffs, then selects A or B. There are no separate criterion winners, numeric ratings or fixed weights. Length, polish and citation count alone do not establish creative quality.

If the judge cannot support a substantive preference, it must say so and choose A by convention. When it does this consistently in both answer orders, each system receives one vote. Identical presented answers are excluded before judging.

Rubric files and configuration

The default is nus_pairwise_v2.txt. The alternative nus_pairwise_v1.txt is selectable through the rubric path in the configuration. Changing that path reuses the answers and creates different judgment cache identities. The selected wording uses the current presentation and response checks; saved runs retain their exact prompts. See configuration examples.

Every pair, in both orders

  1. 50shared questions
  2. 3system pairs
  3. 2judges per pair
  4. 2answer orders

600 judgments total. Each question produces 12 choices. Each system appears in 8 of them, giving 400 comparisons per system across all 50 questions.

Judges receive the question and complete answers labeled A and B, without provider labels or web tools. URL destinations are hidden uniformly; meaningful citation labels and prose remain. Bare links and provider-only citation markers receive neutral source labels. Original answers are preserved in the report. Style and content can still reveal a system’s identity.

Responses must contain a valid A/B choice and a complete comparative explanation. Automated checks reject obvious empty, meta-only or abruptly cut explanations, with bounded retries. These checks cannot verify the explanation’s factual claims.

From votes to a ranking

Win rate=wins ÷ comparisons

Every question, opponent, judge and answer order receives equal weight. Both orderings count even when a judge changes its preferred system after the swap. Exact aggregate ties share a rank. The head-to-head table shows each direct matchup separately, including any preference cycle.

Only questions with all three answers and every required valid judgment enter the main ranking. Exclusions and their reasons are reported; completing fewer questions can change which tasks the ranking represents.

This is a normalized Borda-style win count. Shah and Wainwright (2018) analyze ranking by pairwise wins. Their results provide methodological background, rather than validation of this rubric or these judges.

What the error bars show

The 95% intervals describe how the win rates change when the completed questions are resampled. The default analysis draws 1,000 samples of whole questions with replacement, keeping every question’s answers and judgments together. The same draws produce intervals for differences between systems. Dror et al. (2018, §3.2) discuss bootstrap evaluation.

These intervals hold the saved answers, judges, rubric and calls fixed. They do not include variation from generating new answers, choosing other judges or changing the rubric, and do not establish coverage across all creative tasks.

Checks on judge behavior

A-position preference
How often a judge chooses A, across the balanced presentations.
Swapped inconsistency
How often a judge changes its preferred system when the same answers exchange positions.
Judge disagreement
How often the two judges choose different systems for the same presentation.

Swapped inconsistency can reflect order effects, call variability or the fixed convention for indifference. One call per presentation cannot separate these causes. Agreement does not establish accuracy. Detailed bootstrap ordering fractions are resampling frequencies, rather than probabilities that a system is truly superior.

Scope and supporting literature

The ranking records these judges’ preferences for these answers. It does not measure absolute creativity, verify historical novelty or establish factual accuracy. No human or domain-expert validation of this rubric or judge panel has been performed. Using two provider families does not make their errors independent.

The method draws on the following work:

  • Runco and Jaeger (2012), The Standard Definition of Creativity: originality and effectiveness as central qualities.
  • Boden (2011), Creativity and Art, Introduction, pp. 1–2: novel, surprising and valuable ideas, including unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas.
  • Zheng et al. (2023), Judging LLM-as-a-Judge: position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases in LLM judging. Balanced orders and neutral links do not eliminate every bias.

These sources motivate the design; they do not validate CREST’s specific operational choices. The AI scientist paper comparison shares the overall preference principle, with a different sampling design and uncertainty calculation.
