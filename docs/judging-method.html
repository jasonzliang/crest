NUS rubric

NUS means New, Useful, and Surprising. These qualities guide a judge toward one overall choice between two answers: which makes the stronger creative contribution? The default NUS prompt applies to research answers and scientific papers. The AUT-inspired rubric offers an alternative emphasis on ideas, conceptual range, and originality.

What makes an answer creative?

Suppose the task is to improve a city's response to extreme heat.

New

What does the central idea add? Look for an original claim, mechanism, relationship, or combination. Familiar parts can form a meaningful new contribution.

Example: an answer connects a city's cooling centers to bus routes, explaining how transport access changes who can benefit. Assess that relationship, rather than counting unfamiliar details.

Useful

How well does the idea serve the task? Look for a coherent mechanism, relevant reasoning, and evidence appropriate to the claim and requested development stage.

Example: a cooling proposal explains who can reach each center and when it opens. An exploratory plan can leave tests for later; a claim that it already reduced illness needs supporting evidence.

Surprising

How does the approach depart from the expected answer? Start with what a competent answer would usually propose, then identify a meaningful departure.

Example: instead of only adding cooling sites, an answer changes bus schedules to match peak heat. The unexpected move matters if it improves access; an unrelated futuristic gadget adds little.

These examples illustrate the questions to ask; they do not establish that any proposal is historically novel. New and Surprising can overlap: the same relationship may be both original and unexpected.

Make one overall choice

Why one overall judgment reviews the evidence and tradeoffs behind this design.

The judge explains both answers' strengths, limitations, and tradeoffs, then selects A or B. There are no criterion winners, numeric ratings, fixed weights, or score averages. A surprising proposal with an unexplained mechanism can lose to a more convincing contribution. More detail helps when it supports the idea or fulfills the task; length, polish, repetition, and citation count alone do not establish creative quality.

There is no tie option. If no substantive preference can be supported, the judge must say so and choose A by fixed convention. That choice is a reporting requirement, not evidence of greater creativity. Applied consistently in both answer orders, it splits the votes evenly.

Evidence and limits

The judge uses identifiable prior work from its existing knowledge and does not browse. Failure to recall a precedent is uncertainty, not proof of novelty. Explicit fictional or hypothetical premises are accepted. Invented proposals differ from claims presented as established facts; a false factual claim central to the reasoning weakens the contribution.

Originality and effectiveness are central to Runco and Jaeger's definition (2012). Boden (2011, Introduction, pp. 1–2) describes novel, surprising, valuable ideas and unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas. These concepts inform this rubric; neither source validates this particular comparison by AI judges.

The result records the selected judges' preferences for the supplied answers. No human or domain-expert validation of this rubric or panel has been performed. Zheng et al. (2023) document position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases in AI judging. Agreement between judges does not establish accuracy.
