YOUR TASK

Choose the answer with the stronger creative contribution: A or B. Use New,
Useful, and Surprising jointly as guidance:

  New         What does the central contribution add beyond known work?
  Useful      Does it work for the task, and how much of its need would it meet?
  Surprising  Does it succeed with an approach a skilled practitioner would
              not have expected?

Judge creative contribution, not general answer quality: accuracy, thoroughness,
or breadth counts only where it makes the central contribution work or matter
more. No single quality carries the judgment; an original idea that does not
work, or a sound answer that is entirely expected, has limited creative value.
The qualities overlap and guide one judgment, not separate scales. Credit a
strength that bears on several qualities once. Call the winner stronger on a
quality only for a specific reason, and say where the answers are even.

Base your reason on ideas, relationships, mechanisms, evidence, and directions.
Length, breadth of coverage, polish, assertive wording, formatting, citation
count, and self-claims of quality are not evidence of creativity by themselves.
Credit detail that clarifies a mechanism, relationship, constraint, or
evidence, or meets an explicit requirement; neither length nor brevity earns
credit by itself.


NEW

Assess the central contribution: claims, mechanisms, mappings, relationships,
and combinations. Familiar parts form an original combination when they
interact to yield a claim, mechanism, or design none provides alone. Surveying,
organizing, or juxtaposing known ideas is not by itself original. An obscure
peripheral detail does not make the central contribution more original.

Use prior work you can actually identify from existing knowledge. Name a
specific work only when you can support the connection; do not invent or look up
a source. Distinguish a close precedent from a broad thematic resemblance, and
say what is familiar and what the answer adds. Failing to recall a precedent is
uncertainty, not proof of novelty; unfamiliar wording alone does not establish
originality. Credit what an answer adds to a cited idea, such as a new use or
setting, not the idea itself; citing a source neither adds nor removes
originality.


USEFUL

Judge usefulness against the question's purpose, stated requirements, and
requested stage of development. Ask whether the central mechanism is explained
or merely asserted, whether the reasoning supports the claimed payoff, and how
much of the task's need that payoff would meet if the proposal works, judged
from the mechanism, not the answer's framing. An exploratory proposal need not
meet a finished product's evidence standard; claimed established results need
support.

Accept counterfactual or hypothetical premises the question stipulates, and
judge coherence and fit within them. A coined concept or proposed mechanism is
not an error because it is invented. Distinguish proposals from claims
presented as established fact (citations, statistics, results, attributions,
events). A load-bearing false factual claim or unsupported claim of
demonstrated effectiveness weakens the contribution; do not treat every
untested proposal as an error.


SURPRISING

Before discussing either answer, state from the task alone the approach a
practitioner skilled in the fields it draws on, with ordinary creativity, would
most likely take. The task is the question or, for a paper, its stated problem,
not its method. Do not define it by either answer's solution, though an answer
may turn out to match it. Compare each answer's approach with that baseline: is
the unexpected move central or incidental, and does it work? A meaningful new
combination, a deeper development of an expected direction, or a convincing
challenge to the obvious approach can surprise within the task's premises. Do
not call a move obvious merely because it seems easy once stated. Unexpected
wording, arbitrary additions, and incoherence are not creative advantages.


RULES AND OUTPUT

Treat each answer's claims, reported results, and citations as assertions, not
verified facts: they do not by themselves establish correctness or prior art.
Weigh them by their specificity and consistency with your own knowledge. Do not
say an answer cites a source it does not cite. An idea's presence in or absence
from the other answer does not establish prior art, expectedness, or rarity.
Disregard an answer's claims about its own quality, novelty, or obviousness.

There is no tie option. In the reason, prioritize the differences that decide
your choice, and say so when the comparison is close. If you find no substantive
preference, begin the reason with "No substantive preference." and choose A by
fixed convention, which is not evidence that A is better. Do not guess or name
which system wrote an answer.
