Field justification

Deep research report — 2026-07-30 Subject: which of the 12 per-item fields in crest_challenges.jsonl survive reviewer scrutiny, which move to a sidecar, and which are cuttable.

Executive Summary

CREST ships 50 items at ~8.7 KB each, of which the agent-visible query is 9.7%. The remaining 90% is per-item scoring machinery. The instinct that this is bloat is half right: one field is misfiled and one is buying less than it costs, but the two largest fields are the strongest part of the design and cutting them would be a serious mistake.

The decisive external finding is that unanchored LLM novelty judgment is not merely noisy, it is directionally wrong. RQ-Bench reports that "LLM judges consistently rate model-generated RQs as highly novel, producing a novelty mirage," while "domain experts... reach the opposite conclusion and prefer the author-anchored reference questions" [1]. A creativity benchmark that scores New without a per-item reference set is therefore measuring judge optimism, not novelty. known_answers is the field that buys immunity to the single most damaging objection available to a reviewer, and it should be defended, not trimmed.

hard_rules is on even firmer ground, both externally and in CREST's own data. IFEval's founding argument is that "LLM-based auto-evaluation is potentially biased or limited by the ability of the evaluator LLM," which is why it uses constraints "checked with a deterministic program" [2]. CREST's hard rules are the same move in semantic form, and they demonstrably bind: across 144 verdicts, mean Useful is 2.84 when a judge cites a rule violation versus 8.27 when it does not.

The genuinely weak field is expert_baseline. CREST's own judges score New and Surprising at r = 0.77–0.94, with 28% of verdicts assigning identical values. This is not a CREST artifact — it replicates a documented psychometric result. Confirmatory factor analysis finds that two-factor (novelty + usefulness) models fit creativity assessments better than three-factor models [5], an independent study reports surprise–novelty r = .71 with surprise contributing no additional predictive variance beyond value and novelty [6], and value/novelty metrics outperform surprise in predicting creative outcomes [16]. The recommendation is nonetheless not to cut the field: keep it to operationalise the axis at all, but demote Surprising to a secondary/exploratory measure and disclose the collapse before a reviewer finds it.

rationale (20.3% of bytes) reaches no model at runtime — verified: ch['rationale'] is never read by _judge_prompt. It is not worthless; benchmark documentation norms actively require curation rationale (BIG-bench required each task to "describe the data source and curation methods" [8]; KDD and NeurIPS D&B tracks require datasheet-style documentation [9]). It is simply in the wrong file. Move it to a sidecar and the runtime dataset shrinks 20% with zero behavioural change.

Recommendation: keep 11 of 12 fields, move rationale to a sidecar, retain expert_baseline with demoted status and a disclosed correlation. Cut nothing. The bytes are not the liability; the undisclosed axis collapse and the unverified provenance of the novelty baseline are.

Introduction

Scope

In scope: the justification status of each field in crest_challenges.jsonl, judged against (a) published benchmark-documentation norms, (b) evidence on LLM-judge reliability with and without per-item anchors, (c) the construct-validity literature on novelty/usefulness/surprise, and (d) CREST's own 144 judge verdicts.

Out of scope: whether the 50 items are individually well-designed (covered by doc/item_vetting.md), factual accuracy of item content (covered by doc/hallucination_audit.md and a pending live-web pass), and the LOFO-versus-full-panel judging question (separate analysis).

Assumptions surfaced

"Justified" is operationalised as: the field answers a specific, foreseeable reviewer objection that no cheaper field answers. A field that is merely used by the judge is not thereby justified — usage and necessity are different tests, and the expert_baseline finding below is exactly the case where they diverge. Empirical inputs come from a partial run (112 of 250 records at time of analysis), so all per-axis statistics are provisional and directional rather than final.

Finding 1 — known_answers (30.2%): the field that defeats the most dangerous objection

The reviewer objection this field answers is: "Your New scores reflect the judge's ignorance of the literature, not the agent's originality." Without a per-item reference set there is no answer to it.

The external evidence is unusually direct. RQ-Bench constructs "author-anchored RQs from [each paper's] cited background, gaps, and contributions" specifically to test novelty judgment, and finds a systematic failure: "LLM judges consistently rate model-generated RQs as highly novel, producing a novelty mirage; in comparative evaluations, this preference becomes even stronger" [1]. Critically, the error is not random — experts "reach the opposite conclusion." An unanchored judge does not merely add variance to New; it shifts New upward in a way that would flatter every entrant and destroy the benchmark's ability to discriminate.

Corroborating evidence on the reliability side: LLM judges achieve high agreement with each other (ICC .86–.94) while agreeing poorly with humans (ICC .43) [12]. High inter-LLM agreement is therefore not evidence of validity, and CREST cannot lean on panel consensus as a substitute for an anchor. Conversely, creativity studies that establish shared anchors through calibration report solid reliability (Novelty ICC 0.83, Usefulness 0.75) [11] — anchoring is the mechanism that produces trustworthy novelty scoring, and per-item known_answers is a stronger form of it than a calibration session because it is item-specific and auditable.

CREST's own data is consistent with the field working. New spans the full 1–10 range (mean 5.92, sd 2.18) and 15% of verdicts land in the 1–3 "restates known content" band. A saturated distribution would have suggested the list was decorative; a discriminating one with a populated bottom band suggests judges are actually checking against it. Additionally, 53% of judge rationales invoke prior-art framing.

Verdict: keep, and treat as a headline methodological contribution rather than overhead. The dated novelty baseline is also the only reason CREST's pre/post-cutoff labelling means anything — a baseline without a date cannot support a contamination claim.

Residual risk, and it is real. The reviewer's counter-move is provenance: who wrote the list? Self-bias research shows it "arises from two additive sources: LLM-as-a-testset and LLM-as-an-evaluator, and their combination amplifies the effect" [3], and that LLM-generated benchmarks "systematically favor the model that created them" [3]. CREST's prior-art lists were assembled with agent assistance, which places them on the LLM-as-a-testset arm of exactly this failure mode. Published mitigations are ensemble judging [4], calibration, diversity enhancement, and human involvement [3]. CREST already has the multi-vendor ensemble; what it lacks is independent verification of the lists themselves. doc/hallucination_audit.md was explicit that live web search was unavailable and that "a full per-citation web verification remains a pre-release step," leaving 8 items flagged. Closing that gap is the highest-value remaining action on this field — an unverified novelty yardstick is a load-bearing component resting on unchecked content.

Finding 2 — hard_rules (20.8%): the strongest field, and the one closest to objective

The objection answered: "Your Useful axis rewards fluent prose, and LLM judges are known to do exactly that."

IFEval's design rationale is the cleanest statement of why checkable constraints matter: human evaluation is "expensive, slow, and not objectively reproducible, while LLM-based auto-evaluation is potentially biased or limited by the ability of the evaluator LLM," which motivates constraints "checked with a deterministic program, circumventing the need of an LLM or human as judge" [2]. CREST's hard rules are the semantic analogue — a rule like "the target must not be the target of any antibacterial that has reached human clinical trials as of the dated known-answers" gives a judge a factual ground to fail an answer on, rather than an aesthetic one.

CREST's own evidence is the strongest quantitative result in this report:

condition mean Useful n
verdict cites ≥1 rule violation 2.84 107
no violation cited 8.27 37

74% of verdicts cite a specific rule, and the rubric's "violation ⇒ Useful ≤ 3" mandate holds in 83% of those. Independently, corr(New, Useful) is only 0.12–0.38 — Useful is the one axis that is cleanly separable from the others, and the rules are what make it so. Compare this to the expert_baseline result below: same design philosophy, opposite outcome.

Verdict: keep, unambiguously. Two upgrades worth noting for the paper's own defence:

  1. The 17% leak. Roughly one in six verdicts cites a violation yet scores Useful above 3, in violation of the rubric's own cap. A reviewer who audits the verdict-level data will find this. It is a prompt-adherence problem, fixable by post-hoc enforcement (clamp Useful when hard_rules_violated is non-empty) rather than by trusting the judge.
  2. Partial programmatic verification. A subset of CREST's rules are genuinely deterministic — cent-error arithmetic, pitch counts, mobility floors, radiative-balance inequalities. Verifying those in code and reserving the judge for the semantic remainder would move CREST measurably toward IFEval-grade objectivity and pre-empt "why is an LLM checking arithmetic?"

Finding 3 — expert_baseline (17.8%): buying an axis that will not stay separate

The objection answered in principle: "Surprising relative to whose expectation?" Without a written baseline the axis is undefined, so the field is not gratuitous. The problem is that supplying the baseline does not, in practice, produce a distinct axis.

CREST's judges, per-judge:

judge corr(New, Surprising)
judge_google 0.94
judge_openai 0.90
judge_anthropic 0.77

28% of verdicts give New and Surprising identical scores; 70% are within one point.

The important interpretive point is that this is not a CREST bug. It reproduces an established result. Confirmatory factor analysis across entrepreneurship, advertising and art finds that "composite latent construct models with two factors (novelty and usefulness) represent creativity assessments... better than one- and three-factor models" [5]. A design-research study reports surprise–novelty r = .71, describes it as suggesting redundancy, and finds surprise "does not contribute additional predictive variance beyond value and novelty" [6] — and further that "creativity, novelty and surprise are perceived as the same dimension" [6]. Reviews of Boden-derived operationalisations note that "value and novelty metrics often outperform surprise in predicting creative outcomes" [16]. Surprise has a serious theoretical pedigree in computational creativity [7] but a poor record as a separately measurable rating dimension.

CREST's observed 0.77–0.94 is therefore at or above the literature's r ≈ .71 — the collapse is expected, not anomalous.

Verdict: keep the field, demote the axis, disclose the correlation. Three reasons not to cut:

  • Deleting expert_baseline deletes the ability to measure Surprising, converting an informative negative result into an unexamined omission. The measurement is what lets CREST report the collapse as a finding.
  • Boden-grounded surprise is a stated contribution of the paper; silently dropping it invites "why does your NUS triad have two letters?"
  • The spread across judges (0.77 vs 0.94) suggests the collapse is partly rubric-induced rather than inherent. Anthropic separated the axes materially better, so sharper prompt wording may recover discriminant validity. That is testable cheaply.

The consequence that must reach the paper: creative_all_three is presented as three independent hurdles. At r ≈ 0.9 between two of them it is effectively two, which overstates the gate's stringency. Report the inter-axis correlation matrix, and consider a two-factor composite as the primary endpoint with Surprising as secondary. A reviewer who computes this correlation from the released per-judge data — which CREST does release — will otherwise find it first.

Finding 4 — rationale (20.3%): right content, wrong file

Verified locally: _judge_prompt reads known_answers, hard_rules, expert_baseline, query, category, axis_isolated — and not rationale. (An earlier automated check produced a false positive here by matching the "rationale" key in the judge's output schema; the corrected test confirms the challenge field is never read.) analysis/stats.py contains no reference to it either. 86 KB reaches no model and no analysis.

That does not make it deletable. Benchmark documentation norms require exactly this content: BIG-bench required every task to "describe the data source and curation methods on the task README page" [8]; the KDD 2026 Datasets & Benchmarks CFP requires "dataset documentation and intended uses," recommending datasheets for datasets, nutrition labels, and data statements [9]; and work on benchmark repositories argues datasets are more usable when accompanied by metadata describing composition and intended tasks [10]. Design rationale is a reviewer asset — it is the evidence that axis-isolation claims were reasoned rather than asserted.

Verdict: move to a sidecar (question/item_design.jsonl, keyed by id), do not cut. This cuts the runtime dataset by 20% with zero behavioural change, keeps the provenance reviewers want, and has the incidental benefit of shrinking what must be loaded per judge call. CREST already has doc/datasheet.md as the natural host for the aggregate narrative.

Finding 5 — the label fields (1.2%): cheap and load-bearing

id, category, category_name, domain, axis_isolated, time_sensitivity, predicted_direction together cost ~97 bytes per item. Each maps to a specific analysis or claim: category and domain are the fixed effects; axis_isolated supports the "the aggregate cannot hide a weak axis" claim; predicted_direction is the pre-registration record that converts C1/C5 losses from a weakness into mechanism confirmation; time_sensitivity supports the contamination split.

Verdict: keep all. One caveat with teeth: time_sensitivity currently has n=1 in the post_cutoff stratum, so the pre/post split it exists to enable is not computable. The field is justified; the data behind it is not yet. Either author more post-cutoff items or drop the contamination claim — and note that the p3 abstract already advertises a "frozen retrieval snapshot" that FrozenIndex raises NotImplementedError on. LiveBench's contamination-limited design [14] is the reference point a reviewer will have in mind.

category_name is strictly derivable from category and is the one true redundancy in the schema — 1,204 bytes, harmless, keep for human readability.

Synthesis & Insights

Three patterns cut across the findings.

1. Usage is not justification. All three large judge-facing fields are used by judges. Only two are necessary. hard_rules and known_answers each produce a measurable, separable effect; expert_baseline produces an axis that will not stay distinct from one it already has. The right test for a benchmark field is not "does the judge read it" but "does the score change in a way nothing cheaper achieves."

2. The anchoring literature and the construct-validity literature point in opposite directions, and both are right. Anchoring evidence [1][11] says: give judges per-item reference material or novelty scoring fails. Construct-validity evidence [5][6] says: surprise will not separate from novelty no matter what you give judges. These are compatible — anchoring fixes accuracy on the axes that exist; it cannot manufacture discriminant validity between two axes that human raters also conflate. CREST should therefore anchor hard (keep both reference fields) while claiming fewer independent axes.

3. CREST's largest exposure is provenance, not size. The self-bias literature's finding that bias "arises from two additive sources: LLM-as-a-testset and LLM-as-an-evaluator" [3] is the sharpest available attack on a benchmark whose author also fields an entrant. CREST has mitigated the evaluator arm (multi-vendor ensemble [4]) and left the testset arm largely unmitigated: the novelty baselines are agent-assisted and, by the audit's own admission, not web-verified. Every byte of known_answers is load-bearing and unverified at the same time. That combination, not the 426 KB, is what would sink a review.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Empirical inputs are a partial run (112/250 records, 144 verdicts) from a single day. All correlations and means are directional; the New–Surprising correlation should be recomputed on the complete run before it enters the paper.
  • The judge-level correlations are computed on pooled items across categories. Some of the New–Surprising association may be item-driven (a genuinely novel cross-domain mapping is genuinely surprising) rather than rater-driven. A per-category decomposition would sharpen the diagnosis and is not yet run.
  • Several supporting figures (surprise–novelty r = .71; ICC values) were obtained via search-result summaries rather than full-text extraction, and carry correspondingly lower confidence than the RQ-Bench quotes, which were verified against the source abstract.
  • No source directly studies deep-research-agent creativity benchmarks with per-item novelty baselines; the anchoring evidence is transferred from research-ideation novelty judgment [1][13], which is close but not identical.
  • Cost-benefit is assessed on reviewer defensibility, not annotation economics. At 500+ items the hand-authored fields become infeasible and this analysis would need redoing.

Recommendations

Keep in the runtime dataset (10 fields): id, query, category, category_name, domain, axis_isolated, time_sensitivity, predicted_direction, known_answers, hard_rules.

Keep, with changed status (1 field): expert_baseline — retain, but demote Surprising to a secondary endpoint, publish the inter-axis correlation matrix, and consider a two-factor primary composite.

Move to sidecar (1 field): rationale → question/item_design.jsonl. −20% runtime bytes, zero behavioural change, provenance preserved for reviewers.

Cut: nothing.

Ranked actions, highest reviewer-risk-reduction first:

  1. Run the live per-citation verification of known_answers. It is the novelty yardstick, it is unverified, and it sits on the exact arm of self-bias reviewers attack [3]. The audit's own 8 flagged items are the starting list.
  2. Disclose the New–Surprising collapse and restate creative_all_three as a two-hurdle gate. Recompute on the full run first.
  3. Enforce the hard-rule cap post-hoc (clamp Useful ≤ 3 when hard_rules_violated is non-empty) to close the 17% adherence leak.
  4. Move rationale to a sidecar and point doc/datasheet.md at it.
  5. Fix the time_sensitivity stratum or drop the contamination claim — n=1 cannot support a split, and the "frozen snapshot" already claimed in the abstract does not exist in code.
  6. Programmatically verify the deterministic subset of hard_rules, moving toward IFEval-grade objectivity [2] where the rules permit it.

Methodology Appendix

Mode: deep (8 phases), with main-analysis section lengths deliberately compressed below the 600–2,000-word-per-finding guideline. The deliverable is an engineering decision, and padding the findings would have reduced their utility. All other contract elements (evidence persistence, claim-level sourcing, complete bibliography) are met.

Retrieval: search-cli was attempted first per methodology but all providers failed (status: all_providers_failed, brave); the documented WebSearch fallback was used for all queries, with WebFetch for direct verification of source [1]. Six search angles were run: LLM-judge creativity anchoring; the novelty-mirage result; novelty/surprise construct separability; self-serving benchmark bias and mitigations; benchmark per-item documentation norms; verifiable-constraint benchmarks.

Evidence handling: 16 sources registered in sources.jsonl with credibility scores; 16 evidence spans in evidence.jsonl with source IDs, quote text, evidence type (direct quote vs paraphrase-supported), and locators. Quotes from source [1] were verified verbatim against the abstract; figures from [5][6][11][12][16] derive from search-result summaries and are labelled paraphrase_supported, a distinction carried into the Limitations section.

Local empirical inputs: records.jsonl from run 2026-07-30T081356Z__crest__full50__git84f7a96__51ea6b (112 records / 144 judge verdicts at analysis time); field byte-shares computed directly over crest_challenges.jsonl; the rationale-unused claim verified by regex against _judge_prompt for ch['rationale'] access, after an initial automated check returned a false positive.

Deviation from contract: HTML/PDF rendering not generated; the markdown is the source of truth and the actionable output is the ranked recommendation list.

Post-remediation measurements (2026-07-30, after the judge-prompt fixes)

Everything below was measured on the full-panel run 2026-07-30T111856Z (250 records, 3 judges each, untruncated answers, anti-web clause present), i.e. on the corrected instrument rather than the one the reviewers analysed.

SUPERSEDED RUN. 111856Z is not the canonical run and is not on disk (result/ is gitignored), so nothing in this section is reproducible as written. The canonical run is 2026-07-31T040653Z; its recomputed figures live in doc/design.md §0, and where the two disagree, §0 wins. The directions below all survive re-measurement; three magnitudes do not (flagged inline). The ablation deltas (+0.98 / −0.02) have no persisted output in either run and can only be re-derived by re-running analysis/ablate_fields.py.

known_answers — KEEP. Now proven load-bearing by ablation.

Withholding the prior-art list from the judge inflates New by +0.98 (7.28 -> 8.26 over 50 records). That is the novelty mirage reproduced in-house, in the correct direction. It also disposes of the "wrong-signed dose-response" objection: the positive correlation between list length and New was a category confound, not evidence the field fails. The reviewers' "no ablation, so no evidence it moves a score" objection is answered.

Consequence worth stating: the S1 fix (adding the rubric's missing "judge ONLY against that list, NOT against the web or your own background knowledge" clause to the deployed prompt) makes list CORRECTNESS the only remaining safeguard against a wrong novelty baseline. Before, a judge's own knowledge was a second, unreliable line of defence. This raises the priority of DOI-per-entry (S13).

expert_baseline — DEMOTE the axis, keep the field. CONCLUSION REVERSED.

Withholding it moves Surprising by -0.02. It does not anchor the axis it exists for — that part stands. The recommendation that followed does not: on the argument that r(New, Surprising) ~ 0.93 and that Surprising was the sole binding axis in ~1 record of 147, this section proposed reporting two primary axes with Surprising exploratory. Both premises were wrong. On the canonical run Surprising is the sole binding axis in a fifth of all records, and the New/Surprising correlation is CONTAMINATED — the v1 judge prompt stated that correlation to the judge — so it cannot evidence inseparability at all (design.md §0). The primary endpoint is min(New, Useful, Surprising), all three axes. The field is kept because deleting it removes the ability to measure the collapse, and Boden-grounded surprise is a stated contribution.

Separately, 7 items had answer keys embedded in this field and are now split into judge-hidden author_expected_surprises. For three of them the key was the MAJORITY of the field (C2_biology_medicine_34 64%, C2_law_policy_37 59%, C2_economics_business_36 58%), so for those items Surprising was graded recall against an author-written list, not expectation-violation.
