AUT-inspired rubric

This rubric compares the creative ideas expressed in research answers and scientific papers. It borrows fluency, flexibility, and originality from the Alternate Uses Task (AUT), but produces one qualitative A/B preference—not a standardized AUT score or a measure of the creator’s ability.

Both crest and crest_ais use the canonical files in crest/prompts/. The old AIS rubric paths remain compatibility aliases. Selecting another rubric creates different judging inputs; existing saved judgments and published rankings are unchanged.

What the judge considers

ConsiderationApplication to research outputsLimit
FluencyDistinct, relevant ideas expressed when a shared task explicitly asks for several ideas under comparable submission requirements.This is coverage of the request, not measured generation ability. Raw counts confer no advantage for finished papers or different-topic comparisons.
FlexibilitySubstantively different mechanisms, assumptions, functions, or perspectives that contribute to the task.Extra datasets, section headings, or named disciplines do not automatically add directions. A focused contribution can be stronger than a broad list.
OriginalityUnusual central contributions and meaningful new relationships, judged against identifiable relevant approaches.Unfamiliarity, obscure wording, or an idea’s absence from the other answer does not establish historical novelty or statistical rarity.

Elaboration helps the judge understand what an idea contributes; it is not a fourth score. The judge weighs the central contribution and the whole output, without numeric weights, criterion winners, or an automatic preference for the longest list.

Coherence and evidence clarify what a contribution proposes or establishes. An exploratory hypothesis need not demonstrate a finished system. Hypothetical premises supplied by the question are accepted; an answer cannot assume away the task’s requirements.

What the research supports

  • AUT has several scoring methods. Fluency, flexibility, and originality are common dimensions, but instructions, response validity, scoring level, and aggregation all matter. The adaptation above makes those choices explicit. Reiter-Palmon, Forthmann, and Barbot (2019).
  • Quantity can contaminate quality scores. Benedek and colleagues found that summing originality ratings strongly tracked fluency in their divergent-thinking tasks. Their top-scoring results do not establish a universal best aggregation for research papers. We therefore use no sum or automatic quantity bonus. Benedek et al. (2013).
  • Categories and reference populations matter. Olson and colleagues describe flexibility as categories of uses and statistical originality relative to a response sample. Our two-artifact comparison supplies neither a standardized category system nor a normative sample. Olson et al. (2021).
  • AUT and NUS are compatible concepts. Stevenson and colleagues assessed AUT responses with originality, usefulness, and surprise ratings alongside flexibility. AUT is a task, not a universal formula that competes with NUS. Stevenson et al. (2022).

The particular rules for finished research artifacts are design choices informed by this literature. These studies do not validate this pairwise rubric for scientific creativity or establish that it matches human judgment better than NUS.

Checks used during review

ComparisonRequired treatment
Repeated paraphrases versus independent proposalsMerge repetitions within each answer; judge the remaining contributions.
One developed idea versus a long listExplain the task-relevant tradeoff; neither quantity nor the single cleverest fragment wins automatically.
Both answers satisfy a request for five valid ideasQuantity does not distinguish them; compare their substance.
A speculative mechanism versus claimed demonstrated efficacyApply evidence expectations appropriate to the claim and its development stage.
Papers from different fieldsUse each paper’s own task and field context; withhold unsupported count or breadth advantages.

These are checks of the rubric’s logic, not measured model performance. Human alignment still needs blinded domain-expert judgments on representative outputs, with order, repetition, and length sensitivity checked separately.

Select the AUT-inspired rubric

In a new CREST judging configuration under crest/configs/, set:

rubric: ../prompts/aut_research_pairwise_v1.txt

In an AIS configuration under crest_ais/configs/, set:

"rubric": "../../crest/prompts/aut_research_pairwise_v1.txt"

Other settings stay as needed for the experiment. Paths are relative to the configuration file. Both examples resolve to the same rubric. Prepare new judging inputs when changing methods; do not edit frozen plans or combine NUS and AUT-inspired votes in one ranking.

The response format remains {"reason": "…", "winner": "A"}, with A or B as the winner. The reason explains both outputs and any material comparison limits. The reported win percentages are preferences under the selected rubric, not AUT scale scores.

If no creativity-relevant preference can be supported, the judge must say so and choose A by the existing fixed convention. In two consistent reversed-order judgments, this convention splits the votes; the choices should not be described as substantive evidence of superiority.
